# CS 255: System Analysis and Design

This repository showcases my work in **CS 255: System Analysis and Design** at Southern New Hampshire University. Through the DriverPass case study, I applied systems analysis and design principles to translate stakeholder needs into documented requirements, process models, UML diagrams, and a proposed system design.

The coursework demonstrates my ability to analyze business requirements, model system behavior and structure, evaluate technical constraints, and communicate design decisions to both technical and nontechnical audiences.

## DriverPass Project Overview

DriverPass is a driver-training company seeking a web-based information system to improve how customers purchase training packages, access online learning materials, take practice exams, schedule driving lessons, and monitor their progress.

As the systems analyst, I analyzed stakeholder interviews and business needs to define functional and nonfunctional requirements and develop models for the proposed system. The design also accounts for multiple employee roles, role-based access, security, system availability, and the maintenance of current training content by authorized staff.

## Portfolio Reflection

### What I Did Well

One of my strongest contributions to the DriverPass project was organizing information from the client interview into clear system requirements. The interview included business goals, user expectations, technical preferences, and operational concerns. Separating those details into functional and nonfunctional requirements made the proposed system easier to evaluate and reduced ambiguity before design work began.

I also considered the needs of multiple user roles instead of treating every user the same. Customers, secretaries, driving instructors, administrators, and information technology staff require different permissions and system functions. Accounting for those differences supports a more secure and practical design.

### What I Would Improve

I would strengthen the connection between the written requirements and the system diagrams by adding a formal requirements traceability matrix. This would make it easier to confirm that each approved requirement appears in at least one design element and that every modeled feature has a documented business purpose. I would also continue refining diagram labels and layouts so that relationships remain readable when the models are viewed outside the original design tool.

### Interpreting User Needs

I interpreted user needs by identifying each stakeholder, the tasks that person must complete, the information required to complete those tasks, and the constraints that could affect the experience. For example, the client's need for convenient training access became requirements for browser-based availability, online practice exams, and scheduling. Concerns about employee responsibilities became role-based permissions and administrative controls.

Considering user needs is essential because a technically functional system can still fail if it does not support the way people actually work. Connecting each design decision to a stakeholder need helps prevent unnecessary features, missing workflows, security gaps, and costly redesign later in development.

### My Design Approach

My design process begins with the business problem rather than a preferred technology. I first identify the stakeholders, goals, constraints, and required outcomes. I then classify the requirements, model the system from multiple perspectives, and compare the models for consistency. Process models explain how information moves through the system, structural models define the system's objects and relationships, and behavioral models show how the system responds over time.

For future projects, I will continue using iterative modeling, stakeholder feedback, requirements traceability, and early validation. These techniques make assumptions visible, reveal conflicts before implementation, and create a stronger foundation for software development.
